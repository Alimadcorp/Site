history of me, i guess

I am Muhammad Ali, a 17 year old developer and student from Pakistan. I love making websites, apps, and games, and right now I am most interested in writing secure programs. You might also know me online as Alimad, Alimad Co, or Alimad Corporations—yes, I pretend to have a full-on company that doesn't exist yet, but I sure do hope it does one day!

I was born into a Muslim household on October 12, 2008, in Sharaqpur, Pakistan. From the very beginning, I liked computers. When I was a kid, I completely took over my uncle's computer, staying on it non-stop and rarely letting my brother use it. In fifth class, my cousin Arman intrigued me to join YouTube, and around that same time, I loved making drawings and crafts at home. Naturally, I started a YouTube channel called "Art and Craft Ideas". It went on for about 2 years—nothing crazy popular, just around 400 subscribers—and I eventually deleted it. I don't particularly remember why, but hey, I was a kid, so lol. The only video I ever recovered and republished to my new channel is this origami craft video portfolio.

Then, in 2018, my friend started using the new Microsoft Video Editor that came preinstalled with Windows (back before ClipChamp). He drew a bunch of Minecraft pictures using MS Paint, put them together as an animation on the editor, and we published it on his channel. We made Episode 2 together, and then I took over for Episode 3. Except this time, I spent about 40 days grinding away on Techsmith Camtasia, and finally released it on my new YouTube channel at Minecraft Animation Episode 3 on my main hub, @alimadco.

A few weeks passed by and I was scrolling through YouTube when I saw a video about a new device. The creator showed a 3D model to explain how it worked, and just for a second, I caught a glimpse of the software he used: "Autodesk Maya". My mind was blown—3D editing software actually exists? OMG ITS PAID?? A few days later, I was watching a production by Black Plasma Studios and discovered they used a totally free editor called Blender. It was a dream come true. I opened it up, ran through a ton of tutorials, and went straight to my cousin's house because he had an actual computer. We made our first animation together, but we didn't even know how to export a video! We literally screen-recorded the viewport playback and uploaded it directly to YouTube as our First Blender Animation Build.

From there, I went into deep learning mode with Blender, rendering random animations—including a significant 3D Architectural Model Showcase. I also created casual video series using Camtasia, like my playlist for The Crash Technology Series and my tutorials on the Introduction to Computer Graphics Layout. That was my life for a solid two years.

In 2022, I found a button. It sat at the top right of my Blender editor. I was running Blender 2.79b on my laptop back then because it couldn't support modern OpenGL 3.1 versions. Honestly, if it hadn't been for my laptop being so old, I might have never clicked it, but there it was: "Game Engine". I clicked it, it switched me over to the Blender Game Engine, and out of pure curiosity, I looked up a tutorial. I learned how to build interactive mechanics and launched my very first prototype—a basic local multiplayer goal-scoring game called Makeways, which dropped on July 6, 2022. At first, I shared it via MEGA with friends who wouldn't actually play it, so I switched over to itch.io, publishing it on February 23, 2023, at Makeways on Itch. I spent two more years building games inside BGE, wrapping up with my latest title, Swiveling Escape Engine. In total, I deployed 8 Blender games before migrating to Godot because I needed mobile deployment support—BECAUSE LITERALLY NO ONE PLAYED MY DESKTOP GAMES.

That brought me into the Godot era. My first game was simple, released on July 11, 2023, called Polk Core, quickly followed on July 14 by a fast-paced family favorite bubble-popper called Buggle Mobile Build. Then I did something wild—I ran my first game jam before I had ever even joined one! It was called the Alimad Banana Game Jam, and I developed a submission for my own event called The Minions Resurrection. Around this time, I also picked up Java using Processing, writing a triangle area calculator and a Pendulum Physics Simulation App. I was in 9th class by then, and near the end of the term, balancing this got pretty tough because my parents got strict with me for spending too much time working on online projects.

Even so, I managed to create a highly addictive local multiplayer game in Processing called Madarea Arcade. My final Godot game dropped on September 25, 2023, called Madrun Digital Board Game, which was based on a real physical board game I had crafted by hand a year prior. Eventually, a year later, I realized Godot web exports weren't hitting the mark for native mobile browsers, so I needed a heavier framework.

On March 18, 2024, at exactly 10:00 PM—right after walking out of my last 10th-grade Urdu exam—I put Unity on download on my new laptop. It was Ramadan. At 3:00 AM the following morning, it finally finished downloading. I opened it up, wrestled with the learning curve, and shipped my first test sample: Shootmad Shooter. A few days later, I officially entered a real community competition: the Interstellar Game Jam 6. I submitted Zolitude Space Survival, which received incredibly positive feedback on a global developer live stream review (Live Stream Review Archive). It was Eid-ul-Azha that day, and it marked my first time joining a true community of creators! I even made a devlog video about it at Zolitude Development Log.

Later on, I joined Fox Hollow Jam 2 and dropped a chaotic little title called Banana Prototype, which introduced me to an amazing community of developers. From there, I built Zomboozle for the Zombeez Jam (missed out on their $500 prize pool, unfortunately) and created Ikeamad Game Sandbox for Lame Jam 42. I learned a ton from those failures and made great friends along the way, including developer DataPotato, leading to an awesome YouTube Coding Collaboration Video.

A year later, I jumped into Fox Hollow Jam 3 with my game Chromadicc and actually won the $75 prize! You can see their live reactions and ranking feedback right here on their stream: Fox Hollow Jam Live Judging. Since PayPal doesn't work natively here in Pakistan, I used those direct earnings to buy an official Minecraft account. Shortly after, the host of that jam had to step away from organizing games to take a full-time job, which inspired a reflective community video I made: Silent Game Dev Retrospective. Alongside these, I've kept up my streak with the GMTK Game Jams, tracking from my GMTK Jam 2024 Profile to my GMTK Jam 2025 Submission, and I'm locked in to compete in the upcoming 2026 jam too.

While all this game development was rolling, December 2024 arrived, and we hit a massive administrative roadblock during my first year at college (Government College University, Lahore). Every year, GCU conducts intermediate "Sendup" examinations for roughly 3,000 first-year students spanning morning and evening shifts. The seating plan was distributed as a massive 40-page unsearchable PDF showing roll number ranges for 7 or 8 different exams. You couldn't even use standard text search because your exact roll number wouldn't show up inside a text block formatted as "3600-3712". I decided to fix this. I engineered a Python processing script to parse the PDF structure directly into clean CSV data, then built a static HTML app that calculated a student's exact seating room instantly based on their roll number. I integrated a direct Firebase database config right inside the static code. The app went completely viral—over 70% of the entire college layout used it to find their exams. It even had a vibrant comments section full of positive feedback before the database was cleared. That massive infrastructure project pushed me headfirst into web development, starting out with quick UI mockups in v0 before moving on to master native full-stack logic.

By February 6, 2025, a college classmate introduced me to Hack Club—an incredible global community of teen hackers who build software systems and receive hardware and domain grants for their open-source projects. I dove straight in right after my first-year exams. My initial project was a fast-paced Unity title called "GleamBox". It turned into a huge success, but after working on it intensely every day for two weeks, I hit major burnout and walked away from its potential, even after playtesters asked to join my repository to help expand the engine! I transitioned fully into scalable web apps, earning my first domain grant from Hack Club to register alimad.co Personal Hub. Over the last two years, I have scaled my cloud projects up significantly, and I now orchestrate over 30 custom subdomains across my network (mwahahhahaaa!). My YouTube channel is highly active, while my legacy itch.io profile functions as a cool historical archive.

Through these global developer circles, I made an awesome friend named Finn Awen Nijpels during the Winter Melon Game Jam, where we co-authored the puzzle title *MagnetMad* together. He's an amazing coder and friend, and we still host cooperative Minecraft servers together regularly!

Looking forward, I have officially cleared my FAST-NUCES engineering entry exams with an excellent aggregate score. Inshallah, I will be pursuing my Bachelor of Science in Computer Science (BSCS) from 2026 to 2030, moving deep into core computing departments, expanding my full-stack freelancing portfolio, and hacking on open-source infrastructure projects with Hack Club. I'm staying on the daily grind while enjoying being under 18 until my next birthday rolls around this October. Love and peace :twin:
